\begin{abstract}
We study whether large language models (LLMs) ``know'' more about retrieval
relevance than their outputs reveal. Using linear probes on a single
frozen forward pass over (query, passage) pairs, we compare four
relevance \emph{readers} on the same model, prompt, and answer token:
(i) the model's literal output verdict (zero-shot LLM-judge), (ii) a trained
probe on the final-layer hidden state (the \emph{output representation}),
(iii) a trained probe on the best residual-stream layer (\emph{internal}),
and (iv) a probe on the top attention heads. Across eight models (3B--8B,
LLaMA, Mistral and Qwen, base and instruct), reading internal activations decodes
relevance markedly better than reading the output. With query-clustered
bootstrap confidence intervals and Benjamini--Hochberg correction across the
$8\times4$ family of tests, the attention-head probe beats the
output-representation probe on all eight models---on every LLaMA and Mistral
model and on both Qwen models, with corrected $q<0.002$ throughout
(effect $+0.06$ to $+0.15$). This edge survives a capacity-matched control
that gives the final layer the identical read-out procedure, and the same
internal activations are also far more decodable than the model's zero-shot
verdict. We are explicit about the boundary: re-reading the final layer with a
different read-out (a per-chunk ensemble) matches or beats the head probe on
two of eight models, so the matched-procedure comparison and the margin over
the model's own verdict are the claims we stand behind, not a general
``internals beat the output end''. The residual-stream advantage over the
output representation is narrower: it is individually significant on the three
\emph{base} models
($+0.078$, $+0.064$, $+0.059$) and shrinks on their instruct counterparts,
indicating that alignment moves relevance signal toward the output without
closing the gap. Comparing base and instruct pairs, alignment reliably
improves the output readout (the judge gets stronger), and the model's own
readout direction still extracts less than a probe trained on the same layer on
every model. In short: relevance is encoded internally, and the model's own
readout extracts only part of it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
When an LLM is asked whether a retrieved passage is relevant to a query, its
literal answer is often little better than chance. Yet the same forward pass
may already contain a far cleaner relevance signal in its internal
activations. We ask a focused, mechanistic question: \emph{is relevance
better decodable from a model's internals than from its output, and if so,
is the internal signal causally connected to the output the model fails to
produce?}

This is an interpretability question, not a retrieval-engineering one. We do
\textbf{not} claim a probe that beats a strong cross-encoder reranker, nor
zero-shot cross-domain robustness; prior exploration refuted those framings.
Our contribution is the \emph{relative} science of internals vs.\ output:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Existence.} On the same model/prompt/answer-token, a linear
  probe on attention-head activations decodes (query, passage) relevance
  substantially better than the model's own output verdict, and better than a
  trained probe on the output representation (final hidden state).
  \item \textbf{Attribution.} Decomposing the gap into a
  \emph{training} component (final-layer probe $-$ judge) and a
  \emph{depth/internal} component (best internal layer $-$ final layer) rules
  out the obvious explanation and locates the rest: the training component is
  $0/8$ after correction---fitting a probe on the output representation buys
  nothing over the zero-shot verdict---while the depth component is
  individually significant on the three base models and is compressed by
  alignment.
  \item \textbf{Mechanism.} Relevance decodability peaks in the middle of the
  network and declines toward the output layer. Comparing base vs.\ instruct
  models, alignment \emph{improves} the output readout but does not eliminate
  the internal--output gap.
  \item \textbf{Causality.} We probe the causal role of the internal direction
  with a steering intervention whose verdict we pre-committed to: the internal
  direction has to produce a relevance-\emph{aligned} shift that lands in the
  tail of a random-direction null distribution. We report the outcome in full,
  including the arm where it does not.
\end{itemize}

We report query-clustered bootstrap confidence intervals throughout, apply
Benjamini--Hochberg correction across the family of edges, and are explicit
about which claims are individually significant, which are null, and which
hold on only a subset of models. Delineating effect strength honestly is
central to this paper.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Probing classifiers} read latent features from frozen representations
to test what information is linearly available \citep{alain2017,hewitt2019};
we use them comparatively across depth and against the output.
\textbf{Logit lens and tuned lens} \citep{nostalgebraist2020,belrose2023}
project intermediate states to the vocabulary, motivating our depth analysis.
\textbf{Activation steering / representation engineering}
\citep{turner2023,zou2023,li2023iti} edit residual directions to change
behavior; we use a difference-of-means steering vector for a causal test.
\textbf{Calibration and hallucination} work shows models can encode
correctness they do not surface \citep{kadavath2022,azaria2023,burns2022}.
Most relevant, \citet{orgad2024} show LLMs internally represent truthfulness
they do not output, and concurrent work finds hallucination is linearly
decodable from mid-layer hidden states while output-based detectors stay near
chance \citep{aiersilan2026}. We do \emph{not} claim to be first to observe
that internals outstrip the output; our contribution is to localize this to a
specific, controllable signal---query--passage \emph{relevance}---and to
characterize it quantitatively: a depth-resolved decodability curve, a
decomposition separating ``reading internals'' from ``being trained,'' a
read-out-matched capacity control, a pre-registered causal steering test, and a
base-vs-instruct contrast. \textbf{Relevance mechanism}
work reverse-engineers how neural rankers compute relevance via causal
interventions on attention heads \citep{chen2024relevance}; trained classifiers
are also known to beat prompted LLM judges at relevance \citep{gienapp2025}. We
differ by studying a generative LLM's internal-vs-output gap on relevance rather
than a ranker's internal circuit, and by attributing the gap to depth beyond
training. \textbf{RAG relevance} estimation usually targets reranking accuracy;
we deliberately stay on the interpretability question and do not compete with
rerankers.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Task and data.} We use MS MARCO v1.1, forming (query, passage, label)
triples with relevance labels. Splits are made \emph{by query} (70/15/15) so
no query appears in both train and test, preventing leakage. We report
ROC-\auc{} on the held-out test set.

\textbf{Prompt and extraction.} Every reader sees the identical instruct
prompt, \texttt{"Document: \{p\}\textbackslash nQuestion:
\{q\}\textbackslash nIs the document relevant to the question? Answer:"}, and
all activations are taken at the final \emph{answer token}. Per-head
activations are the \texttt{o\_proj} outputs reshaped to
$[\text{heads}, d_{\text{head}}]$; the residual stream is each decoder
layer's output.

\textbf{Four readers.} (1) \emph{LLM-judge}: the model's own normalized
$P(\text{yes})$ vs.\ $P(\text{no})$ at the answer token, \emph{zero training}.
(2) \emph{Output-representation probe}: an $\ell_2$-regularized logistic
regression on the final hidden state. (3) \emph{Internal probe}: the same
probe on the best residual-stream layer, selected \emph{on validation}.
(4) \emph{Attention-head probe}: top-$k$ heads chosen on validation, their
activations concatenated into one logistic regression. As an untrained
output-side control we also use a \emph{logit lens}: each layer's residual is
projected through the model's own final norm and unembedding to read
$P(\text{yes})$ vs.\ $P(\text{no})$ at every depth, with no training and no
label-based layer selection.

\textbf{Gap decomposition.} We split the total edge over the judge into
$\text{train\_edge} = \text{final} - \text{judge}$ (pure training, both at the
output) and $\text{internal\_edge} = \text{best\_resid} - \text{final}$ (pure
depth, beyond training). The thesis ``internals $>$ output'' requires internal
readers to exceed \emph{both} the judge and the output-representation probe.
A third component is read-out rather than depth: how the final layer is read.
We therefore also hold the read-out procedure fixed (same feature budget, same
validation-selected units, same per-unit standardisation) and, separately,
replace the single linear read-out with a per-chunk ensemble, to separate
``which layer'' from ``how it is read''.

\textbf{Significance.} Because the split is by query, the correct resampling
unit is the query. We report 95\% confidence intervals and one-sided
$p$-values from a \emph{query-clustered paired bootstrap} (5000 resamples) on
frozen test predictions; the best layer is chosen on validation to avoid
selection-on-test optimism. Because we test four edges over eight models, we
additionally report Benjamini--Hochberg $q$-values within each edge family
(8 tests) and run a cross-model sign test on the direction of each edge.

\textbf{Causal steering.} At the validation-selected internal layer we form a
probe-free relevance direction $d = \overline{h}_{\text{rel}} -
\overline{h}_{\text{irrel}}$ (difference of class means, unit-normalized).
During the forward pass we add $\alpha \|h\| d$ to the \emph{output-layer}
residual at the answer token and sweep $\alpha$, reading the judge's
$P(\text{yes})$ and the test \auc. Because a fixed step in $\alpha$ is not a
fixed intervention---directions differ in how strongly they couple to the
readout---we calibrate $\alpha$ per direction so that each sweep point
corresponds to the same \emph{shift in the yes/no logit difference}, and we
report both the intended and the measured shift. This calibration has one
boundary worth stating: when a direction's coupling to the readout is very
weak, matching a large logit shift drives $\alpha$ into a numerically unstable
range (we observe one random direction whose required $\alpha$ reaches
$\pm1266$, where the logits overflow). Such points are dropped and the number
of usable random directions is reported per endpoint ($11$ of $12$ in that
case). To ask whether steering is
relevance-\emph{aligned} rather than a generic bias knob, we track the
\emph{alignment gap} $\overline{P(\text{yes})}_{\text{rel}} -
\overline{P(\text{yes})}_{\text{irrel}}$ separately over true-positive and
true-negative rows: a relevance-bearing direction widens the gap, whereas a
blunt shift moves both groups together. Each direction is then compared against
a null distribution of twelve random unit directions (norm-matched, same
calibration), so ``the internal direction works'' means it must land in the tail
of that distribution---not merely that it moves the mean. The comparison set is
the internal direction, the output layer's own difference-of-means direction,
and the random directions.

\textbf{How the output representation is read matters as much as where.}
The headline edge compares a probe on 20 concatenated attention heads against a
probe on the final residual vector. To separate ``reading internals'' from
``reading differently'' we re-read the \emph{final layer} with the identical
procedure: the same 2560-dimensional budget, the same validation-selected 20
units, the same per-unit standardisation and concatenation. The edge barely
moves ($+0.043$ to $+0.128$, significant on 8/8 models), so the advantage is
not an artefact of dimensionality or of the selection step. But the reverse
control is equally informative: replacing the single linear read-out with a
per-chunk logistic-regression ensemble raises the final layer from $0.536$ to
$0.621$ on LLaMA-3.2-3B---a gain of $+0.085$, the same order as the entire
internal\_edge ($+0.078$). On two models the ensemble read-out of the final
layer \emph{matches or beats} the head probe outright (Qwen2.5-7B $0.683$ vs.\
$0.680$; Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct $0.719$ vs.\ $0.669$, i.e.\ a gap of $-0.050$).
We therefore do not claim that internal readers beat the output end in general.
The defensible claim, and the one the rest of the paper quantifies, is narrower:
\emph{at a matched read-out procedure} the internal layers yield the better
relevance decoder, and against the model's own zero-shot verdict the internal
advantage is large on every model. Both the positive control (matched
procedure: 8/8) and the negative one (a different read-out of the same layer
reverses the gap on 2/8) are reported here so that neither is left for a reader
to discover.

\textbf{The base-to-instruct contrast.} Across all four base$\to$instruct pairs
the same triple appears: the judge improves (the output ``says'' more:
$0.545{\to}0.614$, $0.574{\to}0.630$, $0.579{\to}0.646$, $0.652{\to}0.660$),
train\_edge falls to or below zero (a final-layer probe stops adding anything
over the judge), and internal\_edge shrinks ($+0.078{\to}+0.026$,
$+0.059{\to}+0.013$, $+0.064{\to}+0.020$; Mistral moves the other way,
$+0.023{\to}+0.037$). The reading is that alignment moves relevance signal
toward the output representation without closing the internal--output gap: the
gap does not disappear, it changes channel from residuals to heads.

\textbf{A third model family (Qwen2.5) confirms and extends the pattern.}
Qwen2.5-7B has a stronger zero-shot judge than any LLaMA or Mistral base model
($0.652$ vs.\ $0.545$--$0.579$), suggesting its pretraining already embeds
relevance signals more directly along the output vocabulary direction. Despite
this, reading the attention heads still beats the output representation on both
Qwen models (base $+0.088$, instruct $+0.059$, both $q<0.002$). The
internal\_edge is significant on the base model ($+0.064$, $q{=}0.003$) and
shrinks on the instruct model---the same base-to-instruct compression seen on
LLaMA, replicated in a third family.
The train\_edge is \emph{negative} on both Qwen models ($-0.060$,
$-0.051$): once the native judge is already strong, training a probe on the
final hidden state actually slightly underperforms it. This is the structural
mirror of the LLaMA case---where alignment \emph{moved} signal toward the
output (judge rising from $0.545$ to $0.614$ on 3B), Qwen appears to have that
signal \emph{already present} in the output pathway from pretraining. The
internal--output gap persists regardless.

\textbf{Depth localization.} Figure~\ref{fig:depth} shows relevance
decodability rising into the mid network and declining toward the output layer,
with the judge baseline sitting below the peak; the same shape holds on all
eight models (per-model figures in the artifact bundle). For LLaMA the
strongest heads cluster in the middle (L10--16), for Qwen slightly later
(L17--20), and for Mistral single heads are uninformative in the earliest
layers (L0--L3 at chance) before becoming informative later.

\textbf{The model's own unembedding reads out less than a trained probe
(logit lens).} One might object that mid-network decodability only reflects
what a \emph{trained} probe can extract, not what the model itself could
surface. We test this with a logit lens: at each layer we project the cached
residual through the model's own final norm and unembedding and read the
normalized $P(\text{yes})$ vs.\ $P(\text{no})$---an untrained, output-side
reader at every depth. As a sanity check the last-layer logit lens reproduces
the judge (maximum absolute deviation $0.011$ across the eight models).

Comparing the two readers \emph{at the same layer}---so that neither gets a
free choice of depth---the trained probe wins on all eight models, by
$0.005$ to $0.129$ \auc. The size of that gap is not cleanly predicted by
training regime: it is largest on two base models ($-0.129$ LLaMA-3.1-8B,
$-0.102$ LLaMA-3.2-3B) but also on an instruct model ($-0.117$
Mistral-7B-Instruct), and smallest on an instruct model ($-0.005$
LLaMA-3.2-3B-Instruct) and a base model ($-0.040$ Qwen2.5-7B). What is robust
is the direction, not the split. Two caveats bound what this shows. First, the comparison is
not capacity-matched in the learning sense: the probe is fitted on 5218
training pairs while the logit lens uses a \emph{fixed} direction, so a gap
means the model's own readout direction is not optimised for this task---not
that the information is unreadable by any fixed direction. Second, applying
the final norm at an intermediate layer is itself out of distribution.

We can measure the first caveat rather than assert it, on the FiQA cache where
the two can be compared on identical data at an identical layer. There, the
model's own fixed direction scores $\auc{}=0.453$ at $L9$ with a
query-clustered 95\% CI of $[0.417,0.489]$---\emph{entirely below chance}
($P(\auc\ge0.5)=0.005$)---while a direction fitted on a \emph{different}
dataset (MS MARCO) and carried over with no target-domain fitting at all
scores $0.568$, and one fitted in-domain scores $0.777$. On that dataset a
direction that has never seen FiQA beats the model's own readout, and the
model's own readout is significantly worse than chance.

We are careful not to generalise this. The same measurement on MS MARCO gives
the model's own readout $0.511$ with CI $[0.475,0.546]$---indistinguishable
from chance---and a FiQA-fitted direction carried to MS MARCO scores only
$0.482$, i.e.\ the transfer advantage \emph{reverses}. So the below-chance
result is a property of FiQA, not a universal statement about the model's
readout. What does hold on both datasets, and is all we need, is the weaker
comparison: the model's own readout direction extracts less relevance than a
probe trained on the same layer, and this is a statement about the model's
readout direction being off-task rather than about the information being
unreadable by a fixed linear map.

What survives both caveats is the
\emph{relative} pattern: the same fixed readout
loses far more at intermediate layers of base models than of aligned ones,
which is consistent with alignment having moved relevance signal toward the
output direction (Section~\ref{sec:mech}).

\textbf{Activation patching: the verdict does not follow the internal
representation.}\label{sec:patch} The comparisons above establish that
relevance is more decodable from internal layers. A stronger question is
whether the model's own computation \emph{uses} what is decoded there. We test
this by replacing the answer-token activation at the best internal layer $L^*$
with another test example's real activation, preferring a partner with the
\emph{opposite} label (all 1117 rows received an opposite-label partner), and
asking whose label the verdict follows. Interpolating
$h_{L^*}\!\leftarrow\!\alpha h_j + (1-\alpha) h_i$ keeps the manipulation in the
residual stream's own coordinate system, so there is no cross-layer format
change and no scale mismatch: both terms are in-distribution activations at the
same layer.

Three checks confirm the intervention is sound. Patching a sample with its
\emph{own} activation reproduces the natural verdict bit-for-bit
(max $|\Delta|=0$) on every model, so the hook is inert when the payload is the
identity. The freshly captured activations match the cache row-by-row (relative
mean absolute error $\le0.004$), ruling out a silent row misalignment. And the
payload is not merely noise: replacing $L^*$ with a \emph{random} activation at
matched norm stays near chance ($0.499$--$0.520$).

The result is model-dependent, and the split is informative. On both LLaMA base
models the verdict does not follow the transplanted content at all: as $\alpha$
goes from $0$ to $1$ the \auc{} against the target label moves only
$0.545\!\to\!0.534$ (3B) and $0.563\!\to\!0.550$ (8B), \auc{} against the
\emph{source} label only $0.455\!\to\!0.466$ and $0.437\!\to\!0.450$, and the
relevant-minus-irrelevant gap in $P(\text{yes})$ changes by $0.003$ and
$0.006$. A probe reads $L^*$ at $\auc=0.61$--$0.62$; the model's own forward
pass from $L^*$ onward extracts almost none of it. The instruct variant and
Qwen2.5-7B behave differently, tracking the transplanted activation
progressively ($\Delta$gap $+0.040$ and $+0.176$): on the instruct variant the
target-label \auc{} falls from $0.607$ to $0.392$ while the source-label \auc{}
rises from $0.393$ to $0.608$, and on Qwen the verdict ends up
\emph{anti}-correlated with the target label ($\auc=0.337$) and correlated with
the source label ($0.663$). So ``the model cannot use its own internal
representation'' is false in general---these models' computations demonstrably
can.

\emph{What distinguishes the models?} Not probe strength: the probe's margin
over the final-layer probe is largest exactly where tracking is weakest
($+0.068$ and $+0.058$ \auc on the two LLaMA base models, $+0.031$ on the
instruct variant, $+0.004$ on Qwen), but the ordering within that is not
monotone, so we report the association without claiming it is the mechanism. A
controller measurement makes the picture sharper: we compute the directional
derivative of the model's own verdict with respect to $h_{L^*}$---a local
property of the network \emph{at the activation it actually produced}, so no
editing is involved and the out-of-distribution objection does not apply. Its
cosine with the probe's relevance direction is $+0.008$ on LLaMA-3.2-3B and
$+0.006$ on Qwen2.5-7B, while the spread of the same statistic over sixteen
random directions reaches $|0.037|$ and $|0.035|$; the probe direction sits at
the $0.56$ and $0.38$ percentile of those nulls. The output readout is
therefore, to first order, \emph{not pointed at the direction from which a probe
reads relevance}---and this holds for Qwen too, i.e.\ \emph{even in the model
whose verdict is demonstrably sensitive to $L^*$ content}. That is the
misalignment, measured without perturbing anything: the information is present
and the downstream network uses $L^*$, but the direction the verdict is locally
sensitive to is not the relevance direction.

For comparison, transplanting the same real mid-layer activation into the
\emph{output} layer also fails to transfer discriminability, and---this is the
point of the control---\emph{norm-matching the payload to the target layer's own
norm does not rescue it}: at $\alpha{=}1$ the norm-matched payload gives
$\auc=0.499$ and the unmatched one $0.499$ on LLaMA-3.2-3B, $0.515$ versus
$0.515$ on LLaMA-3.1-8B, and $0.353$ versus $0.353$ on Qwen. An earlier version
of this experiment omitted that control, and the observed collapse could have
been an artefact of injecting a vector an order of magnitude too small; with the
control in place, rescaled and unscaled injections are indistinguishable, so the
collapse is not a scale artefact. (The earlier version patched each example with
its \emph{own} activation and therefore measured a different quantity; its point
estimates differ by $0.01$--$0.02$ from the cross-sample version here and the two
should not be compared numerically.)

The honest caveat is that cross-example patching introduces activations the
network never produced for \emph{this} input, so part of any insensitivity could
reflect the model ignoring out-of-distribution content rather than ignoring
relevance specifically. That caveat does not apply to the gradient measurement
above, which is why we rely on it for the misalignment claim and treat the
patching as corroborating rather than primary evidence. It also cuts against
over-reading the Qwen result: tracking a transplanted activation is not the same
as using relevance, and Qwen's own gradient alignment is what would settle
whether its readout is pointed at relevance.

\textbf{Mechanism: base vs.\ instruct.}\label{sec:mech} Across all four base$\to$instruct pairs,
alignment shows a consistent triple effect: the judge improves (output
``says'' more), train\_edge falls toward or below zero (training a final-layer
probe adds little once aligned), and internal\_edge \emph{shrinks} in three of
four pairs while the head edge persists. Combined with
Table~\ref{tab:sig} (internal\_edge significant on base, compressed on
instruct), the reading is: \emph{alignment moves relevance signal toward the
output representation but does not close the internal--output gap}---it does
not merely ``suppress'' the output.

\textbf{Causality: what steering does and does not show.}
We pre-committed to a directional criterion before running the intervention: for
steering to count as \emph{relevance-aligned}, the internal direction's
alignment gap (the relevant-minus-irrelevant split in $P(\text{yes})$) had to
land in the upper tail ($\ge$95th percentile) of a null distribution formed by
twelve random directions swept over the same calibrated logit shifts. It does not. On LLaMA-3.2-3B the internal direction's gap sits at the $0.55$ and
$0.45$ percentile at the two ends of the sweep, and on LLaMA-3.1-8B at the
$0.33$ and $0.25$ percentile (Figure~\ref{fig:causal}, panel c). Two
independently trained base models therefore agree: at the registered operating
point the internal direction is not a relevance-\emph{aligned} intervention.

The criterion was registered at the sweep ``endpoints'' (the $\pm8$-logit
shifts), and inspecting the full profile shows that this is a weak place to
test: at those points every direction has been pushed hardest, and the
alignment gap is compressed toward zero for \emph{all} of them, so a direction
whose effect lives at moderate strength is structurally invisible there. We do
not move the criterion---post-hoc adjustment is exactly what pre-registration
prevents---but we report the full profile, and it changes the picture for two
of the four models. On Qwen2.5-7B, at a $-2$-logit shift the internal
direction's gap ($0.166$) exceeds the maximum of all twelve random directions
($0.143$), i.e.\ the $1.00$ percentile. On LLaMA-3.2-3B-Instruct the effect is
broader rather than a single point: the gap roughly \emph{triples} over baseline
($0.026 \to 0.083$) and lands at the $1.00$ percentile at both $-1$ and
$-2$-logit shifts. The two base LLaMA models, by contrast, never exceed the
$0.75$ and $0.42$ percentile anywhere in the sweep.

So the honest summary has three parts. At the registered endpoint criterion,
the result is a null on all four models. At moderate strength, two of four
models (both instruct) show a relevance-aligned effect beyond the
random-direction null, while both LLaMA base models do not---a split that runs
in the same direction as the base/instruct split in internal\_edge, though four
models (one a single point, one with only five random directions) are too few
to call it a finding. And the endpoint choice itself was a weakness of our
registered criterion: separating directions works worst exactly where we chose
to test. We report the criterion as registered, and the profile as measured.
The saturation-free calibration is what makes these comparisons interpretable
at all: the three direction families are comparable after calibration (mean
logit shift per unit $\alpha$ on 3B: internal $-0.56$, final $+3.27$, random
$-1.24$; i.e.\ roughly a sixfold spread that the per-direction calibration
removes).

The sweep also exposes \emph{why} the gap fails to grow, which is more
informative than the null itself: the alignment gap shrinks monotonically with
intervention strength for \emph{every} direction, including the random ones
(on 3B the mean random gap falls from $0.0121$ at baseline to $0.0016$ at the
strongest negative shift; on 8B from $0.0164$ to $0.0012$). Broadly perturbing
the output residual compresses label separability rather than sharpening it, so
the internal direction is a blunt verdict knob, not a relevance-aligned one.

What survives is the ablation arm. Pushing the yes/no logit difference negative
along the internal direction collapses the verdict monotonically
($P(\text{yes})$ $0.814 \to 0.691 \to 0.573 \to 0.456 \to 0.393$ on 3B;
$0.886 \to 0.260$ on 8B), and the internal direction is the only one of the
three that does so \emph{without} destroying discriminability: \auc{} stays in
$[0.522,0.546]$ on 3B and moves by $0.007$ on 8B, whereas the output layer's
own direction drives \auc{} down to $0.481$ (3B) and $0.501$ (8B), and the
random directions move the mean without a label-aligned component. So the
internal direction is causally coupled to the verdict, but we cannot show that
it is causally coupled to relevance \emph{discrimination}. The correlational
results above do not depend on this arm, and we prefer to bound them with a
reported null than to rest them on a claim the data does not support.

\textbf{A second in-domain dataset.} To check the gap is not specific to
MS MARCO, we retrain and re-test the readers in-domain on BeIR FiQA
(LLaMA-3.2-3B, 1500 queries, 225 test queries, 1125 test pairs; \emph{not}
cross-domain transfer). Negatives are BM25 hard negatives---top-ranked corpus
passages that are not judged relevant---so FiQA is not artificially easy along
the output channel, which is what an earlier random-negative construction had
made it. On this harder construction the picture is stronger than on MS
MARCO, not weaker. The judge reaches only $\auc{}=0.597$ while the best
internal layer reaches $0.829$ (best\_resid$-$judge $=+0.225$, CI
$[0.188,0.262]$), and the output representation no longer sits near a ceiling
($0.724$). The internal\_edge is the largest in the paper
($+0.099$, CI $[0.066,0.130]$), and the head probe is far ahead of both
judge and output representation ($0.884$; attn$-$final $=+0.161$, CI
$[0.131,0.189]$). The two datasets are therefore complementary in the
opposite direction from what one might expect: MS MARCO provides the harder
\emph{readout} comparison (a strong residual probe close to the output
representation), while FiQA shows the internal advantage widening once the
negatives are hard.
\textbf{A stronger judge does not close the gap.} A reviewer might attribute
the weak judge to a weak prompt rather than to the output pathway. Whatever
the prompt, the judge remains the weakest of the four readers on every model we
tested, and the zero-shot judge on the best-prompted large model
(Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, $\auc{}=0.660$) is still below a linear probe on the same
model's attention heads ($0.669$). Prompt engineering moves the judge; it does
not put the judge ahead of the internal readers.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The relevance signal an LLM needs to judge a passage is present in its
internals and only partially surfaced at the output. The results above support a
specific reading of why, and it is a claim about \emph{misalignment} rather than
about uniform weakening. Three measurements point the same way. First, the
signal is \emph{linearly decodable} at mid-network layers while the model's
\emph{own} readout direction extracts much less from the \emph{same} layer: at
$L9$ of LLaMA-3.2-3B a trained direction reaches $0.61$ on MS MARCO and $0.78$
on FiQA, whereas the model's own direction reaches $0.51$ and $0.45$
respectively (and on FiQA that $0.45$ is significantly below chance). Second,
activation patching shows this is not a matter of format incompatibility: a
mid-layer representation transplanted into the output layer transfers no
discriminability, and rescaling it to the output layer's own norm does not help,
so the failure is not that the vector arrives too small. Third---and this is the
sharpest test---the model's verdict is, to first order, \emph{not locally
sensitive to the direction a probe reads relevance from}: the cosine between the
gradient of the verdict with respect to $h_{L^*}$ and the probe's direction is
$0.008$ (LLaMA-3.2-3B) and $0.006$ (Qwen2.5-7B), against a random-direction
spread reaching $|0.037|$ and $|0.035|$, placing the probe direction at the
$0.56$ and $0.38$ percentile of that null. Crucially this holds \emph{even for
the model whose verdict is demonstrably driven by $L^*$ content}, which is why
we read the mechanism as the readout being pointed elsewhere rather than as the
downstream network being unable to use the representation. Alignment then moves
the readout toward the signal without closing the gap. This connects to
calibration/hallucination findings that models ``know'' more than they assert,
localized here to a specific signal and resolved over depth. The Qwen2.5 family
is instructive: its zero-shot judge is already strong, so the
internals-vs-\emph{judge} gap nearly closes, yet reading attention heads still
beats reading the output representation---the internal--output gap is robust to
how well the output verbalizes relevance.

\textbf{Limitations.} (1) Probing is correlational, and our causal evidence is
weaker than our correlational evidence; the steering and activation-patching
controls are reported in full, including where they fail to separate
competing explanations (see the causality experiments below). (2) We use two in-domain
relevance datasets (MS MARCO, FiQA); we make no cross-domain or
reranking-accuracy claims, which prior exploration refuted. (3) The headline
edge is defined against one specific output-side reader: a single
$C{=}1.0$ logistic regression on the final residual vector. Re-reading that
same layer with a per-chunk ensemble closes or reverses the edge on two of
eight models (see the read-out control above), so ``internal readers beat the
output end'' is \emph{not} what we claim; what holds is the matched-read-out
comparison plus a large margin over the model's own verdict. (4) Absolute
\auc{} values are modest ($0.54$--$0.73$) on MS MARCO; the contribution is the
\emph{relative} internal-vs-output relationship, not a state-of-the-art
relevance classifier. (5) The clean residual-stream comparison
(internal\_edge) is significant only on base models; on instruct models it is
positive but not individually significant, so the strong form of the ``residual
stream knows more'' claim is a base-model result.

\section{Conclusion}
On the same model, prompt, and token, relevance is more decodable from internal
activations than from the output the model produces. The advantage is not
explained by training alone, it is largest under a matched read-out procedure,
and it holds in both directions of the base/instruct comparison: alignment
improves what the model says without making its output the better place to
read relevance from. Internals know more than the model can say.
